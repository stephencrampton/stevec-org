FACE TIME

There are things they don't prepare you for
in the prenatal classes,
like how your face must look
when you have a baby in the "Nick U":

How the cashiers speak in hushed tones in the Starbucks
across from the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit.
How your face becomes a kind of explanation.

The guard at Children's Hospital doesn't bother to ask for your ID
He takes one look at your face and sends you on,
past the children in wheelchairs, past the fish tank, to the elevators.

In the prenatal classes, they didn't tell you that
you could be here, standing next to the lights
and machines and oh-so-many tubes;
that your destiny is to listen patiently as the nurse explains,

    This tube is to help her breathe.
    The feeding tube goes through her nose
    straight to her stomach.
    We were able to get a line
    into her umbilical stump, which is good
    because we can get medicine to her quickly
    if she has any more seizures.


You have entered another world.
You feel like you're scuba diving.
Visibility is low, but you can see the other parents.
You imagine your face must look like theirs, strange and numb.
You don't look them in the eyes.
You've taken a great blow,
so hard that you hang in the water, limp.
Words sink past you like lead weights:
"baby girl . . . breathe . . . feeding tube . . . seizures."

You don't reply to the nurse.
Your face says it all.
